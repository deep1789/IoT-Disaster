# 1. Introduction

River floods remain one of the most frequent and costly natural hazards, and the evidence that their human toll is growing is now difficult to dispute. Satellite observations indicate that the share of the world's population living in flood-exposed areas has risen substantially in recent decades, largely because settlement has expanded into floodplains faster than hazard has changed [@tellman2021]. At the same time, the physical drivers of flooding are shifting in regionally heterogeneous ways: climate change both increases and decreases river floods depending on the regime, season and catchment [@bloschl2019; @kundzewicz2014], and disastrous floods emerge from the coincidence of meteorological extremes, antecedent catchment state and exposure [@merz2021]. For the engineer responsible for a regional warning service, the practical implication is that the hazard cannot be managed through structural protection alone. A complementary and much cheaper lever is time: a forecast that reaches people and operators a few hours before the water does allows evacuation, asset protection, and the pre-positioning of response resources. The economic case for this lever is well documented. Hydro-meteorological services, early warning and evacuation are among the most cost-effective measures for reducing disaster losses [@hallegatte2012], benefit–cost analyses of operational flood warning systems regularly return favourable ratios [@carsell2004; @pappenberger2015; @rai2020; @islam2024], and the value of a warning depends strongly on how its uncertainty is communicated and used [@verkade2011].

The Internet of Things (IoT) has changed what is technically possible at the sensing end of this chain. Dense networks of telemetered gauges, low-power radios, gateways and cloud back-ends now deliver observations of river stage every few minutes from thousands of locations [@atzori2010; @gubbi2013], and the disaster-management literature has repeatedly argued that these capabilities should be organised into end-to-end architectures that connect sensing, communication, analytics and decision support [@ray2017; @poslad2015; @basha2008]. A parallel development is the *digital twin*, a continuously updated virtual counterpart of a physical system that is used to monitor, forecast and test interventions [@grieves2017; @tao2019; @fuller2020]. Earth-system scientists have proposed twins at planetary scale [@bauer2021a; @bauer2021b], and recent work has begun to instantiate flood-specific twins coupling observations with hydrologic–hydrodynamic models or graph networks [@rapalo2024; @roudbari2024]. Machine learning has meanwhile become a serious operational tool in hydrology. Recurrent networks trained on many catchments outperform catchment-calibrated conceptual models in a range of settings [@kratzert2018; @kratzert2019; @kratzert2019b], they extend to extreme events and ungauged basins [@frame2022; @nearing2024], and they have been deployed in regional operational flood-warning frameworks [@nevo2022].

Despite this progress, three gaps stand between published models and a warning service that a regional agency could operate on its own IoT gauge network.

**Gap 1: the target of the forecast.** Most hydrological learning work reports skill on discharge or stage as a time series, summarised by Nash–Sutcliffe or Kling–Gupta efficiency [@nash1970; @gupta2009; @knoben2019]. These aggregate measures are dominated by the long stretches of quiescent flow and say little about the quantity a warning duty officer needs: *how fast is the river rising, and when will it cross the threshold at which damage starts?* The rise rate (the stage gradient) and the time to threshold are the operational variables, and the metrics that matter are event-based (probability of detection, false-alarm ratio, critical success index, lead time) and economic rather than squared-error based [@schaefer1990; @roebber2009; @richardson2000].

**Gap 2: the sensors are assumed to be perfect.** Nearly all of the studies cited above are trained and evaluated on curated archives. Real IoT deployments, by contrast, produce dropouts, stuck readings, spikes, calibration drift and delayed packets at appreciable rates [@ni2009; @sharma2010; @teh2020]. A model that has never seen a missing input cannot be expected to degrade gracefully when a headwater gauge goes offline in the first hour of a storm, which is exactly when its information is most valuable. Sensor-fault handling is usually treated as a separate pre-processing stage, so the downstream model's robustness is never quantified.

**Gap 3: the uncertainty is not decision-grade.** Deep ensembles, Monte-Carlo dropout and quantile heads give uncertainty estimates [@gal2016; @lakshminarayanan2017; @klotz2022], but their calibration is rarely verified under the temporal and spatial shifts that characterise deployment, and it is typically not connected to the alerting rule. Distribution-free conformal prediction offers finite-sample coverage guarantees under exchangeability [@lei2018; @romano2019; @angelopoulos2023], and its extensions to time series and distribution shift [@gibbs2021; @zaffran2022; @barber2023] are promising, yet their value for *threshold-crossing alerts* on river networks has not been examined systematically. Warning agencies must also manage the false-alarm ("cry-wolf") problem [@barnes2007; @trainor2015; @leclerc2015], which calls for a principled way to trade detection against false alarms.

A further practical obstacle concerns *topology*. Graph-based hydrological models require a river network, i.e. the knowledge of which gauge lies upstream of which, together with reach lengths. In many data-poor regions such information is incomplete, outdated or not digitised at the resolution of the gauge network. Hydraulic routing, from the classical Muskingum formulation [@cunge1969; @todini2007] to modern hybrid models, encodes the physics of flood-wave propagation but requires parameters that must be calibrated reach by reach. It would be valuable if the essential physical content of routing (that disturbances travel downstream with a finite celerity, so upstream gauges carry predictive information at lead times set by travel time) could be extracted from the telemetry itself.

## 1.1 Contribution

This paper addresses the three gaps within a single, deployable framework which we call **PG-FaultTwin**, a fault-tolerant, physics-guided digital twin for stage-rise and gradient early warning on IoT gauge networks. Its contributions are as follows.

1. **A gradient-first forecasting target and decision-centred evaluation protocol.** The twin forecasts the full 1–24 h stage trajectory and derives from it the rise rate and the threshold-crossing probability. We evaluate with event-based verification against official National Weather Service flood stages (probability of detection, false-alarm ratio, critical success index, lead time, false-alarm episodes per site-year), with relative economic value across the cost–loss spectrum, and with conventional accuracy measures reported separately for quiescent and active conditions.
2. **A topology-free physics prior.** Upstream–downstream relationships, travel times and flood-wave celerities are estimated from lagged cross-correlation of the gauge telemetry alone. They define (i) which upstream gauges feed each forecast point and (ii) a linear multi-lag routing model, an ARX/Muskingum-type transfer function, whose prediction the neural component corrects. We check that the inferred celerities are physically plausible and that the trained network's reliance on neighbours agrees with the inferred travel times.
3. **Fault-tolerant learning.** A stochastic sensor-fault curriculum (random and block dropouts, stale tails, lost channels) is applied during training together with explicit observation-mask channels, and a causal, lightweight edge quality-control gate screens spikes and stuck readings. We quantify robustness under six fault families (14 settings), including dropout rates up to 70%, flat-lining, spikes, calibration bias and packet latency of up to 12 h.
4. **Decision-grade uncertainty.** Quantile heads are conformalised (CQR) on a held-out year, and adaptive conformal inference is run online. We evaluate coverage by horizon, by hydrological regime and under spatial transfer, and we convert the calibrated upper bound into an alerting rule whose false-alarm/detection trade-off is explicit.
5. **A large-scale, fully open benchmark with two generalisation protocols.** We assemble 99 USGS telemetered gauges in four hydro-climatically different regions of the United States (Mid-Atlantic, Ohio Valley, Texas Hill Country, Upper Midwest), covering ten water years and about 44.8 million raw 15-minute readings. Evaluation uses a strict chronological split and a leave-one-region-out protocol that tests transfer to a region never seen in training.
6. **Edge feasibility.** We report parameter counts, latency and memory on a CPU-only device, together with the effect of post-training dynamic quantisation, to show that the forecasting component can be hosted on a gateway.
