# 2. Related work

## 2.1 IoT architectures for disaster management and flood monitoring

The idea that networked sensors can transform disaster response is almost as old as the sensor-network field itself. Early deployments such as the Honduran flood-monitoring system of Basha and co-authors combined in-situ gauges with model-based detection to raise warnings in remote basins with intermittent connectivity [@basha2008]. Subsequent surveys organise the field by the functional layers of an IoT stack (perception, network, processing and application) and by disaster phase (mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery) [@ray2017], and propose semantic, interoperable architectures that fuse heterogeneous sources for crisis management [@poslad2015]. The general IoT literature supplies the enabling technologies: ubiquitous identification and sensing, low-power wide-area connectivity, and cloud platforms [@atzori2010; @gubbi2013]. Two themes are particularly relevant to the present paper. The first is *edge computing*, which moves computation to gateways close to the sensors to reduce latency and bandwidth and to maintain operation when backhaul links fail during the very events they are meant to monitor [@shi2016; @satyanarayanan2017]. The second is *tiny machine learning*, which pushes inference onto microcontroller-class hardware and has been surveyed extensively for IoT settings [@dutta2021; @abadade2023; @rajapakse2023]. Most IoT-for-disaster papers, however, evaluate the communication or architectural layer and treat analytics as a black box. Where analytics are examined, evaluation often consists of a single station or a short record, and robustness to sensor faults, which is the defining characteristic of field IoT, is seldom quantified.

## 2.2 Sensor data quality and fault handling

Field-deployed environmental sensors fail in characteristic ways. Ni and colleagues catalogued the fault types encountered in real sensor-network datasets, including short-duration spikes, constant ("stuck-at") readings, noise and calibration offsets [@ni2009], and Sharma and co-authors showed that these faults are prevalent in real datasets and that simple detectors based on rate-of-change, flat-lining and cross-sensor consistency catch a large part of them [@sharma2010]. A systematic review of sensor data quality confirms that quality is multi-dimensional (accuracy, completeness, timeliness, consistency) and that most work treats it as a pre-processing step [@teh2020]. General anomaly-detection techniques [@chandola2009; @liu2008] can be applied, but they usually require labelled or representative data and do not account for the hydrological context in which a sudden rise can be either a spike or a genuine flash flood. A separate line of work handles missing values by learning imputations or by modelling missingness directly inside recurrent networks [@che2018; @cao2018; @du2023]. Such methods are typically evaluated on imputation error rather than on the downstream forecasting and alerting skill, and they presuppose that faults have been flagged. Our approach differs in two respects: faults are injected into the *forecasting* task and quantified in terms of alert skill, and the network is trained with the mask as an explicit input so that it learns to rely on whichever sensors are alive.

## 2.3 Machine learning for streamflow and flood forecasting

Reviews of deep learning in hydrology document rapid growth over the past decade [@sit2020; @tripathy2024; @zounematkermani2021; @mosavi2018]. Long short-term memory networks [@hochreiter1997] trained jointly on hundreds of catchments reproduce rainfall–runoff behaviour better than calibrated conceptual models, and the benefit comes from learning regional regularities that single-catchment models cannot access [@kratzert2018; @kratzert2019; @kratzert2019b]. Variants handle several time scales with one network [@gauch2021], analyse what hydrological concepts the hidden states encode [@lees2022], quantify predictive uncertainty [@klotz2022], and extend to extreme events [@frame2022] and to ungauged watersheds at global scale [@nearing2024]. Operational deployments have started to appear: a regional framework based on LSTMs and linear models with inundation mapping is in service for flood warnings [@nevo2022]. Attention-based and transformer models have been applied to streamflow [@rasiyakoya2024; @lim2021], and interpretable spatio-temporal attention networks have been proposed for flood forecasting with upstream stations [@ding2020]. Within the general time-series literature, the strongest recent baselines include temporal convolution [@bai2018], basis-expansion networks [@oreshkin2020], probabilistic autoregressive RNNs [@salinas2020], patch-based transformers [@nie2023], and long-sequence transformers [@zhou2021], although simple linear models can match or exceed transformers in long-horizon forecasting benchmarks [@zeng2023]. This finding is consistent with our experience that, at 1–24 h lead times along a river network, much of the predictable signal is captured by linear lagged relationships between gauges, and that the value of nonlinear learning lies in the rapidly rising, rare cases.

Most of these studies use precipitation or reanalysis forcings as the primary input. A telemetry-only forecast, one that uses only upstream gauge measurements, is a weaker information set for catchments with short response times, because it cannot anticipate rainfall that has not yet reached the channel. It is nevertheless an important operating regime: it remains available when the weather feed is delayed or absent, it is the natural design for sensor networks in data-poor regions, and it provides a lower bound on skill that precipitation forcing can then improve. We return to this point in Section 7.

## 2.4 Graph-based and network-aware models

River networks are naturally graphs, and graph neural networks have therefore been applied to streamflow. Graph convolutional networks [@kipf2017] and diffusion-convolutional recurrent networks [@li2018], first developed for traffic forecasting, motivate hydrological variants such as graph convolutional GRUs for hourly streamflow [@sit2021], directed-graph networks for multi-step daily streamflow [@liu2022], and graph networks coupled with digital-twin visualisation for flood forecasting [@roudbari2024]. Graph WaveNet showed that an adaptive adjacency matrix can be learned from data when the graph is unknown [@wu2019]. Those methods either require the network topology to be supplied or learn a dense latent adjacency with no physical constraint. In contrast, we estimate a *sparse, directed* neighbourhood from the lag structure of the telemetry, which is physically interpretable (the lag at maximum correlation is a travel time) and can be audited against known geography.

## 2.5 Physics-guided and hybrid learning

Physics-informed and physics-guided learning combines scientific knowledge with data-driven models [@raissi2019; @karniadakis2021; @willard2022; @reichstein2019]. In hydrology, the debate has emphasised that process knowledge remains valuable even when networks are highly accurate [@nearing2021], and concrete proposals include mass-conserving recurrent architectures [@hoedt2021] and physics-informed learning for river forecasting [@bhasme2022]. Muskingum-type routing is the classical way to encode wave attenuation and lag between river sections [@cunge1969], and mass-conservative formulations have been derived [@todini2007]. Our physics prior is deliberately modest: a data-estimated multi-lag linear transfer function acting on upstream gauges, which is the structure implied by linear routing, with the neural network learning only the *residual*. The design has three advantages: it guarantees that the network starts from a physically sensible baseline, it improves data efficiency, and it provides a transparent baseline that operators already understand.

## 2.6 Digital twins for floods and the environment

Since its introduction in product-lifecycle management [@grieves2017], the digital twin has been adopted in manufacturing [@tao2019] and, increasingly, in infrastructure and environmental systems [@fuller2020]. Earth-system twins promise interactive, observation-driven simulation at planetary scales [@bauer2021a; @bauer2021b]. At the regional scale, twin-like flood systems have been demonstrated that couple hydrologic and hydrodynamic models with observation streams in data-scarce regions [@rapalo2024] and that attach graph-based forecasting to visual analytics [@roudbari2024]. These contributions focus on architecture and on forecasting accuracy in selected basins. What is largely missing is a quantitative treatment of the *twin's behaviour under imperfect observation*, namely how state estimates and alerts degrade when the physical layer misbehaves, and a calibrated link between the twin's uncertainty and the decision to alert. We adopt the functional definition of a twin as a synchronised, queryable virtual model that supports monitoring, prediction and what-if analysis, and we examine the three properties that matter for an IoT deployment: synchronisation robustness, calibrated prediction and decision support.

## 2.7 Uncertainty quantification and conformal prediction

Probabilistic forecasts should be evaluated by proper scoring rules and by calibration and sharpness [@gneiting2007a; @gneiting2007b; @brier1950; @murphy1993]. Quantile regression [@koenker1978] provides a direct route to prediction intervals but offers no guarantee of finite-sample coverage. Split conformal prediction wraps any predictor to give marginal coverage under exchangeability [@lei2018], and conformalised quantile regression adapts interval width to local difficulty [@romano2019]; a recent monograph-length introduction is available [@angelopoulos2023]. River stage data are not exchangeable: they are autocorrelated, non-stationary and subject to regime change. Adaptive conformal inference adjusts the target miscoverage online to maintain long-run coverage under shift [@gibbs2021], extended with aggregation or expert methods for time series [@zaffran2022; @xu2021], and a general theory describes the cost of non-exchangeability [@barber2023]. In hydrology, uncertainty estimation for deep rainfall–runoff models has been compared across approaches [@klotz2022], but guarantees for event detection have received little attention. We contribute an empirical study of conformal intervals for gradient and threshold alerts in a multi-region setting.

## 2.8 Forecast verification and the value of warnings

Verification of warnings differs from verification of continuous forecasts. The probability of detection, false-alarm ratio and critical success index summarise a 2×2 contingency table [@schaefer1990], and the performance diagram displays them jointly with bias [@roebber2009]. For rare events, precision–recall analysis is more informative than ROC analysis [@davis2006; @saito2015]. Economic value in the cost–loss framework quantifies the benefit of a forecast to users whose protective cost is a fraction of the avoidable loss [@murphy1977; @richardson2000; @wilks2001], and has been used to compare flood forecasting systems [@pappenberger2015; @verkade2011]. Social-science research shows that false alarms erode public response [@barnes2007; @trainor2015; @leclerc2015], although the relationship is nuanced, and that warning systems must be judged as socio-technical systems [@sorensen2000; @basher2006]. Operational river forecasters in turn face a distinctive set of challenges including data assimilation, uncertainty communication and the maintenance of observation networks [@pagano2014]. The verification suite of this paper builds on these strands: event-level POD/FAR/CSI and lead time, false-alarm episodes per site-year, precision–recall summaries and relative economic value.
